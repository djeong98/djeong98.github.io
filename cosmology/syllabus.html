Syllabus

ASTRO/PHYS 545: Cosmology — AI-assisted scientific reasoning

Term: [Semester, Year] | Meetings: Tue/Thu, 75 min | Enrollment: 8 | Instructor: [Name] | TA: [Name]

1. What this course is

This is not a lecture course with AI added to it. The premise is that AI systems now produce cosmology derivations, code, and literature summaries of high apparent quality, and that the scarce skill is no longer generating such material but judging whether it is correct.

The course trains one loop, repeatedly:

Think → Ask AI → Inspect → Challenge → Verify → Revise → Synthesize → Ask the next question.

In the first six weeks you apply it to problems whose answers are known. In the project that runs through the second half of the term, you apply it to a question nobody has answered.

Use AI freely; it is encouraged and unrestricted (§12). Whatever route you take, you are expected to defend at a blackboard anything that appears in work you submit under your name.

What you check AI against changes through the term, by design. In the foundation weeks you have the course lecture notes: a document that is correct, against which an AI derivation can be compared line by line. In the map weeks you have papers instead, which are authoritative in a weaker sense and disagree with one another. In the project weeks you have your own map and nothing else. The written reference is withdrawn in stages. The instructor is not: the standard in the room is the same in week 15 as in week 1, and the capstone is where you find out whether it has become yours.

The work in the foundation weeks is therefore not to reproduce the notes. It is to read them, and then to solve eight problems that the notes make possible without making easy.

One thing to settle at the outset. Cosmology has a short list of very well measured numbers and it is easy to mistake them for the subject. Consider what the precision is hiding.

We know the dark matter density to a per cent and we do not know what dark matter is. We know the dark energy density better than that and have no account of it whatsoever. We knew the baryon density to a per cent for twenty years while being unable to say where most of the baryons were, and that accounting closed only recently. And “the CMB is a blackbody” is a statement about a bound, measured in 1990, not improved since, and sitting three orders of magnitude above the distortion that \(\Lambda\)CDM predicts and nobody has been funded to look for.

Each of these numbers is a statement about what the universe contains or how it began, and the reason to know it to three digits is that the third digit distinguishes between things that could be true. A parameter carried without that content is not knowledge of anything, and it is the part of the work an AI already does better than you.

2. Learning objectives

By the end of the term you can:

  1. Foundational knowledge. State and derive the core results of modern cosmology at the level required to judge whether an AI-generated derivation is correct.
  2. AI-assisted scientific reasoning. Define a problem, form your own approach, delegate appropriately, verify the result, and locate and repair the errors.
  3. Literature to knowledge map. Read a body of papers and extract the intellectual structure of a subject: what is claimed, what was measured, what is assumed, and what would overturn it.
  4. Research initiation. Identify a genuine gap, convert it into a research question, and execute the smallest piece of evidence that moves it forward.

The goal is not a finished independent researcher in fifteen weeks. It is a student who can begin an independent scientific investigation.

A specific list of what you should be able to do, organised by topic, is on the Topics and Objectives page.

4. Class format

Every class from week 2 onward runs the same structure. It does not change between phases; only what goes on the blackboard does.

Before class

You read the assigned material and you solve problems. In the foundation weeks the material is the course lecture notes, which are correct and complete; nothing is gained by writing them again. In the map weeks it is a bounded set of papers. No textbook is assigned, though you should own one (§14).

The instructor distributes, one week ahead: the topic and its reading, eight problems (four for Tuesday and four for Thursday), and any additional references.

One submission per week, due Monday at noon. It covers all eight problems. Submissions are timestamped, and the instructor reads the opening lines before Tuesday.

Monday’s deadline is for the written solution. Being able to stand at a blackboard and produce it is separate work, and it happens in the days before each class: solved once, digested twice.

Monday, noon Solutions due, all eight problems
Monday to Tuesday Digest Tuesday’s four
Tuesday Class
Wednesday No class. Repair what Tuesday exposed
Wednesday to Thursday Digest Thursday’s four
Thursday Class
Thursday to Friday Repair what Thursday exposed
Friday Final version due

In the map weeks the cadence changes. One subject per session rather than one topic per week, so there are two of everything: two paper sets, two maps, two deadlines.

Monday, noon Map due for Tuesday’s subject
Tuesday Class
Wednesday, noon Map due for Thursday’s subject
Thursday Class
Friday Final versions of both, with their repair histories

The two lines without AI are attached to each map, not to the week.

Two lines are written without AI, one at each end of the submission.

Opening: the point in your solutions you were least able to verify. Eight such lines take a minute to read and show the instructor where the class is collectively unsteady before anyone reaches the board, which is where the stress test is aimed.

Closing: where AI was wrong, incomplete, or confidently vague this week, and how you found out. If it was not, say so; that claim is testable at the board. If you worked some problems without AI, note which.

These two lines are where your judgment rather than your output is visible, and they are the only written record the course keeps of how you work.

Preparing for the board. Everything you present is written on the blackboard by hand, in eight minutes. Deciding what earns a place there is the central act of understanding in this course, and it is the one step AI cannot do for you: a solution you did not compress yourself does not fit on a board. Nothing about this preparation is collected. The board is the assessment.

In class (75 min)

Orientation 5 min
Board segment (×4, 15 min each) 8 min presenter; 3 min designated challenger; 4 min instructor
Synthesis 10 min

The four problems of a session are not independent. They are one argument cut into four segments, so each takes the previous one’s result as input and the blackboard fills left to right into a single complete structure. Thursday’s first segment takes Tuesday’s last result.

Orientation. Five things, a minute each:

  • Submissions checked in. This happens before the draw, and the draw does not exclude anyone who failed to submit.
  • Problems drawn, roles confirmed.
  • How today continues from the last session. On Thursdays this is not narrative: the first segment takes Tuesday’s blackboard result as its input.
  • The result today’s chain arrives at.
  • Where next week goes.

Nothing about where the difficulties lie. Naming a trap in the first five minutes retires the segment built around it.

Synthesis. Three things, briefly: which of today’s statements are physical and which are choices; where the class was collectively wrong, which the instructor can see from eight opening lines and one blackboard and nobody else can; and where this appears in research.

Synthesis is about the reasoning that happened in the room, not about the physics. If the physics was wrong, that belongs in the repair. A synthesis block that becomes a corrective lecture defeats the format, and its brevity is what keeps it from becoming one.

Class time is the only resource that cannot be reproduced at a desk: eight people and a blackboard in one room. Nothing that works equally well alone is scheduled into it.

Roles

Each class has 4 presenters and 4 challengers. All eight students have a role in every class. Roles alternate between Tuesday and Thursday: Tuesday’s presenters are Thursday’s challengers, and conversely.

Which of the four problems you receive is drawn at the start of class. You therefore prepare all four regardless of role.

Presenter. You reproduce the argument at the blackboard, state where each assumption enters, and mark what remains unresolved in your own understanding.

Challenger. You raise the weakest step and press it. A question that merely asks for repetition does not discharge the role.

Instructor. The instructor takes an apparently harmless line and asks what it costs: Is this true, or true under an assumption you did not state? Is this a physical statement or a coordinate statement? Does it survive outside matter domination? Where exactly did general relativity enter?

Getting stuck is course material, not failure. When a presenter stalls: 30 seconds alone, then the floor opens, then the instructor intervenes. The point at which the argument broke is recorded and becomes the starting point of that week’s repair.

After class

Final version due Friday. Across the week your solutions pass through three states: what you submitted Monday, what Tuesday exposed, and what Thursday exposed. You repair them after each class and submit once at the end.

The final version carries its own history: changes marked or shown as a diff, with two or three sentences on what was wrong and why you had not caught it. That history is the graded part. A solution that was wrong and correctly repaired stands above one that was cautiously vague and needed no repair.

Two or three times in the term the repair is done in class instead, with the instructor watching. What you ask AI immediately after being told an assumption of yours is wrong is the subject of this course, and it is not visible in a submitted file.

The board is photographed each session and archived. By the end of the term the class holds a worked record of roughly a hundred and twenty problems, presented and defended by the people who solved them.

5. Week 1: orientation

The only week the instructor lectures.

Reading: Ch. 1. Physics review, natural and Planck and astronomical units, index notation.

Conventions fixed here are used for fifteen weeks: \(\hbar=c=k_B=1\) and how to restore them, the relation between \(m_{\rm pl}\) and the reduced \(M_{\rm Pl}\), the signature, and the sign conventions for the curvature tensors. Sign errors later in the term are traced back to this list.

Tuesday. Lecture. A practice problem is distributed:

Estimate the temperature at which the universe became neutral. Two numbers are available to you: the binding energy of hydrogen, 13.6 eV, and the ratio of photons to baryons, roughly \(10^9\). Use both, and say what each one does. Then say what you would need in order to do better than an estimate.

Not graded. It is a rehearsal for the submission format, including the two lines, and for what happens at the board.

Thursday. The instructor works the problem at the blackboard under the rules of §4, and is challenged. Nothing about the format is explained that can be shown instead.

6. Weeks 2–6: Foundation

One topic per week across both meetings. Tuesday establishes the setup and the core derivation; Thursday extends and interprets it.

Week Topic Notes
2 FRW geometry and kinematics Ch. 2, §2.1–2.2
3 Friedmann equations and expansion history Ch. 2, §2.3
4 Age, horizons, and distances Ch. 3, §3.1–3.2, §3.4
5 Thermodynamics in the expanding universe Ch. 4
6 Inflation Ch. 6

Two sets of topics are deliberately absent.

The classical cosmological tests (redshift drift, number counts, Olber’s paradox, Alcock-Paczynski, optical depth) are applications of the week 4 machinery rather than foundations of it, and are set as a problem set instead of taking class time.

Everything downstream of equilibrium thermodynamics (freeze-out, dark matter relic abundance, nucleosynthesis, recombination, linear perturbation theory) enters in the map phase as literature rather than as derivation. This is the largest single choice in the syllabus. The foundation phase exists to make you able to judge, not to cover the subject: five weeks buys the geometry, the dynamics, the observables, the thermal machinery and inflation, which is enough to read a paper on any of the omitted topics and tell whether it is right.

Computation. At least one problem a week requires a numerical or symbolic calculation, verified against something you derived independently. The question is never whether the code runs. It is whether you can establish that a result you did not compute by hand is trustworthy, and it is the same question the final project asks with the answer unknown.

Problem sets

Three, in weeks 2, 4 and 6, with the due date announced when each is assigned. These are separate from the weekly eight and they exist because of a limit in the class format.

A board segment is eight minutes, so nothing that cannot be reproduced and defended in eight minutes can be one of the weekly problems, and the weekly set never asks you to sustain a calculation across an afternoon. A problem set does. The criterion is simply that it does not fit on a blackboard.

One problem, not a set of exercises. The workflow is fixed:

Step 1. Before AI, roughly ten minutes, written. The structure of the problem, your expected strategy, the equations you think you need, and what you do not know. Submitted as written, uncorrected.

Step 2. Work with AI. No restrictions.

Step 3. Solution. AI may draft it. You verify and correct it, and you submit it under your name.

Step 4. Interaction reflection, written without AI. What you first thought the strategy was; what you asked AI to do; what it contributed that you had not considered; where it was wrong, incomplete, or confidently vague; what you changed and how you knew to change it.

Step 4 is where this is assessed. Step 3 is not. Step 1 exists so that Step 4 has something to compare against.

7. Weeks 7–9 and 12–13: Knowledge maps

Ten sessions, ten subjects, one subject per session and two a week. The instructor supplies a bounded paper set, two or three papers, for each. The map replaces the problem set as that session’s deliverable.

Two subjects a week is a heavier reading load than the foundation weeks and a lighter writing one. The paper budget is what keeps it feasible, and it is stated rather than suggested.

The goal is not three paper summaries. The goal is the structure of the subject: paper-centric → idea-centric.

The subjects

Weeks 7–9. Constituents of the universe, in order of how well we know them.

1 Photons FIRAS, and the spectral distortions nobody has looked for
2 Baryons \(\Omega_b\) from two independent routes; the lithium problem
3 Neutrinos \(N_{\rm eff}\); the mass sum, where cosmology meets oscillations
4 Dark matter The WIMP argument, and what direct detection has done to it
5 Dark energy \(w\), and what is being assumed to measure it
6 Initial conditions Seeds from inflation; what the data constrains

Weeks 12–13. Current and future probes.

7 DESI, HETDEX Distances and clustering from spectroscopy
8 Roman, Euclid, LSST Shapes from imaging: three designs for one measurement
9 LVK, LISA, PTA Strain: sirens without a ladder, and backgrounds as a thermometer
10 SPHEREx The statistics of the field itself

The first block is ordered by decreasing knowledge, so the sessions open progressively: by week 9 you are in territory nobody has settled. Each subject there ends with a physical claim and the measurement standing behind it. The second block is what will sharpen those claims or break them, and it closes where the first block ended, on the initial conditions.

Your project topic, settled at the end of week 9, need not come from this list, and the four subjects of weeks 12–13 are not offered as candidates. The list exists to give you a field to practise on, not to constrain what you work on.

The four segments

The board accumulates into a map. Segment 1 writes the claim at the centre; segment 2 draws the arrows from what was measured to what was inferred; segment 3 marks which of those arrows are contested; segment 4 marks what would cut them. The photograph at the end is the map.

  1. The claim. What does this subject assert about the universe, stated as a physical proposition that could be false. A number stands behind it; say what the number is a statement about and what its precision buys.

    Reporting \(\Omega_{\rm dm}h^2 = 0.120 \pm 0.001\) is reporting a number, and any AI will do it faster than you. The claim is that the universe contains something that clusters and does not otherwise interact, that it was already non-relativistic when the modes we observe entered the horizon, and that nothing in the standard model does this. The number says how firmly.

  2. The measurement. What was physically measured to obtain that number. Where does raw data end and modelling begin.

  3. The assumptions. What must be true for that inference to hold, and who has questioned each one.

  4. The threat. What would falsify the claim, and what is the strongest current challenge to it.

Segment 3 cannot be done without segment 2, and segment 4 cannot be done without segment 3. Separating claim from measurement from assumption is most of what it takes to read an observational paper.

For a subject that is largely settled, segment 4 asks where the settling stops and what depends on that boundary. Finding the frontier of a solved field is harder than finding the controversy in a contested one, and most real research gaps live there.

Attribution

The failure mode of this phase is not error but fabrication. AI produces fluent literature summaries containing citations that do not exist, claims attributed to papers that concluded otherwise, and positions abandoned a decade ago presented as current. In the foundation weeks the notes catch this. Here nothing does.

Two requirements follow.

Every claim attributed to a paper must be locatable in it. The standing question at the board is “where in the paper,” and the answer is a section or figure number.

The presenter reproduces one figure from one of the papers on the blackboard by hand, with axes, labels, and the approximate position of the data. Drawing a figure requires having looked at it.

Challenger. In the foundation weeks the task is to find the weakest step. Here it is to find a claim that the cited source does not support.

Synthesis. Which assumption on today’s board appeared more than once. Usually one assumption holds up half the subject, and it is visible only after all four segments are up.

Literature budget. Each map has a stated paper budget, and you are not required to follow reference chains beyond it. Knowing when to stop reading and start thinking is under training here as much as anything else.

8. Weeks 10–11: Project clinic and prototype

The instructor is away. Class meetings continue on the normal schedule, run by the students with the TA moderating. You arrive with the research question approved at the end of week 9 and you leave with a prototype.

Clinic sessions (4 meetings). Two students per session put their question and prototype plan on the board; the other six challenge. Every student is on the board twice. The format is the one practised for nine weeks and it runs without the instructor.

Weekly progress report to the TA, who uses AI to triage completeness, conceptual problems, unstated assumptions, questionable claims, and next steps, and returns written feedback.

The prototype is small by design. Reproducing one published figure; a background or simple Boltzmann calculation; a toy parameter scan; reproducing an existing constraint; a simple likelihood; a numerical check of an analytic approximation; a small subset of public data.

The assessment question is not “did you write the code yourself.” It is:

Can you use AI to construct a scientific calculation and establish that its result is trustworthy?

You have been answering this all term: in week 3 against the Friedmann equations, in week 4 against three distance measures, in week 5 against the thermal history, in week 6 against slow roll. The difference is that here nobody knows the answer.

Due at the end of week 11:

  1. Your revised research question, with an account of what changed since week 9 and why
  2. The smallest piece of evidence that would move it forward, and whether your prototype produced it
  3. The prototype, with the checks you ran on it

Item 2 is load-bearing. Two weeks with a research topic and an AI reliably produces a six-month programme. The standing challenger question in every clinic is: which part of this can actually be finished in two weeks?

9. Weeks 14–15: Capstone symposium

Four sessions, eight talks. 20 minutes presentation, 15 minutes questions.

The talk is not judged on how much you produced. It is judged on whether it is a research talk:

  • Here is the field.
  • Here is what we know.
  • Here is what we do not know.
  • Here is the question I identified.
  • Here is why it matters and why it is still open.
  • Here is what I tried.
  • Here is what I learned, including what failed.
  • Here is what I would do next.

The last item is why the survey sessions of weeks 12–13 come where they do. “What I would do next” answered with reference to data that will exist within a few years is a plan; answered otherwise it is a wish.

A prototype that failed and is correctly diagnosed scores above a prototype that succeeded and is not understood.

10. Assessment

Component Weight
Weekly problems and maps, with their repairs 25%
Class participation: presenter, challenger, attendance 30%
Problem sets (3) 15%
Final project: question, prototype, capstone talk 30%

Weekly work. Correctness in the final version is a floor, and by then everyone should reach it: you have watched the material worked twice at a blackboard. The grade is carried by the repair record and by the two lines written without AI. A solution that was wrong and correctly diagnosed scores above one that was right and unexamined.

Participation. Board segments are assessed on three axes: what you chose to put on the board, whether you reproduced the argument or transcribed it, and how you handled the questions. Errors made and worked through at the board are not penalised. Reciting a memorised solution is. The challenger role is assessed on whether the question you raised located a real weakness.

Problem sets. The interaction reflection carries the majority of the weight.

Final project. Weighted across the week 11 brief, the prototype, and the capstone talk.

11. Attendance

This course is student presentation and challenge. Attendance is not encouraged; it is required. With an enrolment of 8 there is no slack: if you are not in the room, one of the four problems has no presenter and a classmate covers it. The cost of an absence is paid by the other seven before it is paid by you.

Attendance credit. Every student begins the term with full attendance credit. If you are absent and the draw assigns you a problem, you lose one credit. Absence on a day you were not drawn carries no deduction, because the class absorbed it.

Covering a missing presenter. The segment goes to a volunteer, who gains one attendance credit. If no one volunteers, it is redrawn among those present. Nobody in this room is ever unprepared for a problem: challengers prepare all four exactly as presenters do, so stepping in costs you an expectation, not a night of work.

Arranged absence. Notify the instructor before the Monday deadline and you are removed from the draw. No credit is lost, and you owe one additional presenter slot later in the term. Illness, research travel and comparable obligations are arranged absences: the distinction is notice, not justification.

Threshold. Three lost credits make participation unrecoverable at a passing level and require a meeting with the instructor before you continue in the course.

12. AI policy

AI is allowed here without restriction, and I encourage you to use it. Solutions, derivations, code and literature analysis may be AI-generated in whole or in part.

If you would rather work a problem out on your own, do that. It is a good way to spend an afternoon and nothing is lost by it. The course is not interested in which route you took; it is interested in whether you can defend where you arrived. Say in your closing line which problems you did which way.

A few problems name AI use as part of the task, because what is being examined there is the verification of something you did not derive yourself. Those say so.

Every submission carries your name and your responsibility.

AI is not used for two things: the opening and closing lines of each weekly submission, and the interaction reflection in each problem set. Those are the places where your judgment rather than your output is visible, and delegating them removes the only written evidence the course collects about how you work. Everything else is checked at the board, where AI is not available to you.

On defending your work. Anything submitted under your name may be put to you at the board without warning. The only workable strategy is to understand what you submit.

14. Books

The reading for each week is the course lecture notes. No textbook is required and none is assigned.

You should nevertheless own one. This is a course about deciding whether a claim is correct, and that is difficult with a single source. When an AI derivation and the lecture notes disagree, what settles it is a third authority that was written by someone else, for a different audience, with different conventions. Having one on your desk, in a subject you will keep working in, is worth more than which one it is.

Choose by your background and your taste. The following are all credible.

Recent

A Course in Cosmology: From Theory to Practice (D. Huterer). Broad and current, and closest to the order this course takes.

Modern Cosmology (S. Dodelson and F. Schmidt). Linear perturbation theory in detail and done well. The closing sections on statistics are also very good.

Cosmology (D. Baumann). Described to me as a modernised Kolb and Turner. If you prefer theoretical clarity, this may suit you.

Particle Cosmology and Astrophysics (D. Hooper). Better suited to students coming from particle physics.

From the 2010s

Cosmology (S. Weinberg). A great book: insightful, accurate, clear. One caveat, that the notation differs from most others. Contains an analytic approximation for the CMB power spectrum.

Physical Foundations of Cosmology (V. Mukhanov). The chapters on nucleosynthesis, inflationary perturbations and the CMB power spectrum are excellent. Everything is done as analytically as it can be.

The Primordial Density Perturbation (D. Lyth and A. Liddle). Focused on how density perturbations are generated and how they become observables, including primordial non-Gaussianity.

Galaxy Formation and Evolution (H. Mo, F. van den Bosch, S. White). Essentially everything from inflation to galaxy formation. The galaxy formation chapters are particularly useful.

Classics

Principles of Physical Cosmology (P. J. E. Peebles). The classic. The classical cosmological tests are explored particularly well, and the later sections are organised by subject so you can move between them.

Cosmological Physics (J. Peacock). A good reference for the wider subject, including AGN and galaxy formation.

The Early Universe (E. W. Kolb and M. S. Turner). The classic text on early universe theory and the thermal history.
